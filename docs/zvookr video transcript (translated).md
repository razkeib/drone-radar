Zvook is an idea for creating affordable sensors capable of detecting various targets using sound. We started by accepting the challenge: the airspace of Ukraine is filled with enemy threats—initially cruise missiles, and later, Shahed drones. Our concept was to detect them using affordable acoustic sensors that we began mounting on mobile operator towers.

These detection methods had been used before, but our element of innovation was using artificial intelligence to quickly "teach" our sensors to detect new types of targets that were previously unseen. For example, regarding Shaheds, we immediately involved volunteers. We organized the collection of acoustic data from the sensors we began mounting across Ukraine along the flight paths of enemy assets. These groups of people listened to the collected data. Initially, of course, these were mostly not enemy flights, but as the AI models learned, we were able to filter out the majority of ambient noise, which made their work much easier. To this day, the number of people involved in this is much smaller.

The AI model is used for identification—to understand if it is an aerial target at all—and then an additional step for its classification. In the end, we get an answer to the question: is it a helicopter, a jet engine, a drone, or a propeller-driven aircraft? The detection fact, along with the coordinates of the detection point and the audio file, is transmitted to the situational awareness system, Virage Delta. We are ready for further integrations if there is such a request.

Actually, it all started when the 125th Separate Territorial Defense Brigade of Lviv was tasked with protecting critical infrastructure objects from aerial threats. Consequently, the brigade commander, Lieutenant General Artur Horbenko, decided to create a joint working group to develop the idea of one of the Lviv IT specialists, Pavlo, regarding the creation of such acoustic sensors. Both I and our current project CTO, Maksym Rudenko, joined this group. Maksym performed the tasks of programming and organizing this complex infrastructure—infrastructure that would be easily scalable because the goal was global; the idea was to cover all of Ukraine, not just Lviv.

Why sound? Because it is a very cheap, accessible, and easily scalable method. Our sensors turned out to be very affordable; they can be mass-produced, and scaling production is also relatively easy compared to traditional radar methods for detecting aerial targets. Furthermore, the co-founders of this project from the company Cher played an important role, as they had experience creating AI models for working with sound. This combination of military personnel and civilian IT specialists produced the result we see now.

Currently, we have 180 acoustic sensors operating across Ukraine. Each is capable of detecting aerial targets at distances of up to 10 km. We take 5 km as an average figure, which tells us that one sensor covers 75 square kilometers of Ukrainian land, totaling 14,000 square kilometers currently covered by our sensors, which detect aerial targets 24/7 and transmit them to the situational awareness system.

This is our main, now-classic acoustic sensor. Based on such devices, we are building our aerial target detection network using mobile operator towers. This sensor consists primarily of a parabolic mirror 50 cm in diameter; it acts to focus the sound onto a microphone. The microphone is located at the focal point, and the signal amplification is two to three times. After that, the sound from both mirrors enters the sensor itself, which consists of a microcomputer. AI models run on this microcomputer to perform the classification.

This device works 24/7 and is protected from weather conditions. We are considering switching from plastic boxes to aluminum ones, which are more protected and impact-resistant for mobile sensor implementations. Connection to a single network happens through mobile operator networks; we are building our network based on them. All mobile operators in Ukraine are present, and they ensure the operation of these sensors in a single, secure, military-standard network. We anticipated all encryption and security because, from the very beginning, we planned to transfer the constructed network to the balance sheet of relevant structures.

The main idea we incorporated when developing the project was decentralization—you can't put all your eggs in one basket. Therefore, as far as the operation of sensors is concerned, in addition to the wired connection from mobile operator base stations, we can use 3G/LTE networks, and we are also currently using the possibility of connecting to Starlink to create isolated clusters of acoustic sensors. Radio channel data transmission is not something we are currently using, but we are building our clusters of acoustic sensors even beyond any internet connection.

60% of the components are imported, and according to our assessment, 40% are produced in Ukraine. Most of the components that are not yet in high-series production are still produced by 3D printing, which is convenient for us because we continue to improve the design itself, and many elements continue to change—which would be difficult to launch into serial production. But I think that perhaps this year or next year, we will reach the stage of manufacturing those components and mounts by casting instead of 3D printing. The mirror is manufactured by contractors in Ukraine; sun and weather protection is also manufactured in Ukraine by our enterprises; the mounts for mobile operator towers are fully manufactured by contractors. Accordingly, soldering and the like—the final assembly—is all done in Ukraine.

The heart of the microphone block is, of course, the microphone. We buy it in Ukraine; it is not produced here; it is an import. We spent a lot of time selecting the specific characteristics of the microphones and stopped on those that allow us to capture the sound of enemy objects in the widest range. Overall, we designed the block to be modular because we want the acoustic sensor network we built to not have high operational maintenance costs in the future. After all, the microphone is vulnerable to the external environment. We have protected it as much as possible; at the moment, in winter or autumn, during heavy precipitation or frost, there are no problems with the operation of the sensors. And if that arises in the future, I think not sooner than 2–3 years, then replacing the microphone block itself is very easy—we planned for that modularity.

The entire component base currently used in acoustic sensors can be manufactured in Europe or Ukraine based on the capabilities available to us. For us, at the moment with our allies, this is an economic question—from China, it's just cheaper. But if the task is set for us to ensure components are entirely from allied countries and not risk being limited by countries like China or others, we are capable of doing that. It is a question of, I think, six to eight months of development, setting the task, and manufacturing our own boards.

Since the microcomputer is not intended for outdoor installation in unprotected environments, we, of course, have to protect the microcomputer in a hermetic box. It needs to be mounted in such a way that it doesn't overheat—which was a challenge for us—and at the same time is protected from the environment. So, the inside of our sensor is what we call "large-assembly." There is also a lot of soldering work there; there are 24 different components inside the box, not counting others.

The sensor consumes very little—10 watts of power at 5 volts—so they work perfectly, receiving power even from simple solar panels. The concept is mobile operator towers because they are located at distances of up to 10 km from each other on average in Ukraine, making it the simplest method. We also divide this project into deployment phases. Phase one is to cover all mobile operator towers. Phase two would be to densify and, if necessary, programmatically reduce the sensitivity of each sensor to get more accurate positioning if we need it. These can be very dangerous flight paths for enemy aerial targets; we can place them closer there if necessary to get more accurate positioning.

Thus, we can install them not only on mobile operator towers but anywhere where there is mobile 3G/LTE coverage—mobile internet. We can also use so-called mesh network construction, where we can transmit detection facts via radio channel. We see these clusters as important for stationary air defense positions. Currently, the command has a vision for developing add-ons that would add the ability to perform accurate positioning. This is using microphone array technology. We see the possibility of providing azimuth information and using it for better targeting of Shaheds for mobile fire groups, or transmitting this azimuth information to other robotic systems, such as anti-aircraft spotlights or interceptor drones.

These initiatives are currently being proven effective. We continue to test them, and therefore "Zvook" is not only about these sensors you can see, but also about developing new types of sensors. They help increase the effectiveness of air defense in places where deploying classic radar systems is impossible, such as close proximity to enemy forces. Thus, our radar systems are exposed to detection by enemy electronic warfare (EW) assets, and the enemy tries to destroy them. "Zvook" sensors simply win there due to their invisibility, as they cannot be detected by EW assets; they are passive by nature—they don't emit anything, they only listen.

Also, it is economically disadvantageous to fight them; there is little armament that can compare with the cost of an acoustic sensor—it's 500 dollars, or at most an FPV drone. But for an FPV drone, the task of detecting this device is not necessarily equipped with mirrors; accordingly, without a mirror, it is very difficult to detect it in rugged terrain. Therefore, we are currently also focusing on installing acoustic sensors close to the line of combat contact; there they become irreplaceable. Radar systems are not there.

It can also be said that for assets that are challenging for radar systems—that is, assets that have a low amount of metal parts, they can be radar-transparent—the acoustic method of detection is effective, and they also remain loud. Shaheds are poorly seen on radar systems at long distances, so at close ranges, at low altitudes, we detect them very effectively and accordingly transmit them to the integrated detection system.

Our plan at the moment is to legalize such detection assets in the legal field because it is still something new, unknown before. For example, military units do not have the ability to accept such aerial target detection assets onto their balance sheets. Therefore, we need to overcome this task now. We need the sensors that are already installed, and those that will be installed this year, to be transferred to the balance sheet of some state structures that would continue our work in the future, be responsible for detecting those targets and transmitting them to the situational awareness system, and accordingly continue to support this network.

Because this is our brainchild, we don't want it to become irrelevant even after victory, because you need to be always ready. No one knows what types of weapons will appear tomorrow, and the acoustic method of detection with our models can be a quickly learning method. For example, if we hear any changes in drone designs, it is enough for us to take a week or two at most to collect the minimum required amount of new sounds of these assets and retrain our models, immediately downloading the updated model to remote corners of Ukraine, to the devices themselves.

The key advantage, I would say, over other approaches to building such networks is that we tried to create a system that was very cheap to maintain. For example, the sensors that were installed two years ago in the test areas from which we started—we still haven't traveled there and haven't done anything with them. They are standing; they have been working for two years. And these are sensors that were assembled, one might say, in a garage—a "garage" type of production at that time. Therefore, after that, we only improved the production process and added modularity. As I mentioned, the modularity of microphone blocks that will be easy to replace in case of failure, because it is still an environment; we have many complex weather conditions in Ukraine, and it sometimes leads to microphone degradation or failure. Therefore, we estimate the need for any maintenance of an acoustic sensor at no more than once every 3–5 years. Such maintenance should be absolutely affordable, and considering the small number of sensors themselves, these will be small costs.

We are increasing production. At the moment, with the presence of funding, we can manufacture 100 sensors per month. To cover all of Ukraine, the project could be broken down into phases. Phase one would be mobile operator towers, and 8,000 would be enough to fully cover the entire Ukraine. Next, we would have to focus on which areas need more detail and installing a larger number of sensors there with greater density. For this, an additional 2–3,000 sensors might be needed, no more. So the whole project could be implemented in a year or two.

We no longer see structural changes that would need to be made; new challenges are no longer arriving, and we are simply continuing to scale. At the moment, the only thing is that the team that has gathered is so capable of giving birth to new solutions that we have additional vision on how to continue to give birth to new products. So we can not only solve the task of detecting aerial targets on the territory of Ukraine on such a strategic scale between cities, as they fly—we would like to build a so-called synergy of sensors. We would like to combine our solution with other manufacturers of detection assets. These can be electronic intelligence detection assets; we can integrate between them. This would help enrich the information we get from such systems—that is, 1 + 1 is not always equal to 2. If more types of detection assets work, they can additionally confirm each other. Thus, further reducing the number of false alerts.

For example, a simple example: Shaheds do not emit anything in the radio range. But Lancets, for example—they are not as loud as Shaheds, they emit in the radio range, but they are much harder to detect by sound. And so, if you focus only on one thing, we will simply lose what we could get from such a synergy of sensors. Therefore, in an ideal world, it would be necessary to build and expand this network, which Zvook is currently building, in the next steps with additional types of sensors, integrate their work, and use our team's R&D to create additional means of processing this information that will be received. We can help other teams. We have already started communicating in this direction, especially after the IT Arena. Thus, we see a lot of work still ahead of us, so we are not resting on our laurels; we continue to work.
